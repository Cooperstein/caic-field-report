CAIC · field report concepts

Is this field earning its place?

Every field is a toll booth between a person who saw something useful and the forecaster who needs to know it. A field earns its place only if what it returns is worth what it costs to collect. Below is each field in the combined CAIC and Avalanche Canada set, judged on that one test.

Cost is what the field takes from the reporter: typing costs far more than tapping, jargon costs more than plain language, and anything that asks for identity or precision before the person has invested effort costs more than the same question asked later.

Value is what a forecaster can actually do with it — filter on it, trust it, or act on it. A field that produces confident-looking but frequently wrong data has negative value, because it takes review time and can mislead.

What the evidence actually supports

These are well-established directional patterns in form research. The magnitudes vary a lot by context, so treat them as reasons to test rather than as numbers to quote in a meeting. Ask me and I will pull real citations for anything here.

  • The count of required fields predicts abandonment better than almost anything else. This is the single most reliable finding in the literature, and it is the reason a "yes" answer should never cost more than a "no".
  • Typing costs far more than tapping, especially on a phone with cold hands. Every field you can turn from an input into a chip row is a real reduction in effort.
  • Asking for identity before the person has invested anything raises abandonment. This is why checkout flows ask for payment last. Contact fields belong near the end, after the report is written.
  • Optional fields sitting among required ones slow everyone down, because each one has to be read and dismissed. Separating them — by screen, by grouping, or by clear marking — is worth more than deleting a couple of them.
  • Prompts change what people write. A labelled blank box gets one line. The same box with the six questions beside it gets a usable observation. Your NAC screenshot is doing the heavy lifting here.
  • Multi-step can beat one long page — but only when the steps are genuinely short. Splitting a long form into long steps is worse than leaving it alone.
  • Never ask a person for something an instrument already measures better. Temperature, wind speed and precipitation totals are measurements, and your weather stations beat a recreational reporter's recollection every time.
  • Precision you cannot collect is worse than none. If reporters cannot reliably tell your categories apart, a finer scale does not capture more signal — it manufactures false precision, and a forecaster cannot tell a careful answer from a guess. Two options a person can answer honestly beat five they cannot.
  • People satisfice. Whatever is easiest to answer is what you will get. If "no red flags" is one tap and "yes" is twelve, your dataset will drift toward "no" regardless of what the snow did.

How to find out what your people actually want

None of the above tells you what Colorado backcountry users prefer. This does, and it takes about a week.

  1. Recruit eight to ten real observers — a mix of frequent reporters, people who have submitted once and never returned, and a couple who have never submitted at all. That third group is the one you are actually trying to win, and the one most likely to be missing from your feedback so far.
  2. Give each of them a real observation to report from a photo and a short scenario, so everyone is entering comparable content. Rotate which option they see first, or the first one will always look hardest.
  3. Watch, and time it. Do not ask which one they like — ask them to think out loud while they do it. Record time to submit, where they hesitate, what they ask you about, and every field they skip.
  4. Count the drop-offs, not the opinions. The useful signal is where someone stalls or gives up, not which screen they say looks nicer. People are unreliable about preference and very reliable about friction.
  5. Then check the quality of what came out. Have a forecaster read the submissions blind and rate whether each one is usable. The shortest form that still produces usable reports is the winner — speed alone is not the goal.

One prediction worth testing. If the audit above is right, the biggest single win available is not the layout at all — it is making the free-text box easy to start. It is the highest-value field on the form and the highest-cost one, which means it is where the leverage is. Try one version with the NAC prompt list and one with a bare box, and I would expect that difference to swamp the difference between these three layouts.
